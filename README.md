# ITEMBA NET

The [ITEMBA NET White Paper](WHITEPAPER.md) defines the direction of this project: a sandboxed, object-centric search engine for every kind of media, with independent and unrestricted discovery, at most one hundred candidate locations per query, and ad-free access inside an isolated sandbox. The architecture, principles and phases in the white paper guide future development. The [Implementation Plan](IMPLEMENTATION-PLAN.md) sequences that work into phases with exit criteria.

**ITEMBA NET** combines three distinct systems paradigms into one cohesive platform:
1. **`itemba-scout` (Focused Web Crawling & Headless Stream Verification)**
2. **`itemba-vault` (Remote Browser Isolation & Pion WebRTC Streaming)**
3. **`itemba-shield` (Triple-Layer DNS, CDP Network, & DOM Ad Sanitization)**

## Movie search

Search by movie title or keywords, optionally adding a release year. **Worldwide is the default.** Global web discovery has no country or preferred-provider bias. Results contain individual movie/player pages and rank successful playback samples, reachable media and matching pages ahead of unchecked listings. The check server's country is evidence about that observation; it does not determine the search region or establish worldwide access. Users can explicitly choose a country preference. Guides, reference pages, merchandise, theater tickets and obsolete Showmax links are excluded.

**Full-length movies** is enabled by default and prefers movies of at least 40 minutes. Known shorts, trailers, previews and menu files are excluded; unknown runtimes remain explicitly unconfirmed. A sampled native runtime overrides a conflicting catalog runtime. Turn the preference off to include shorts. The search API accepts `fullLengthOnly` (default `true`); full-length and all-length cached reports are separate.

The app opens with live **Popular** chart suggestions and a **Recent store releases** view, replacing fixed classic-film examples. Selecting a title runs the normal global search for free and paid watch options; the app does not automatically search or play a title. Suggestions use Apple's public movie charts across the same eight sampled markets, with market and check-time provenance. Recent suggestions use reported store catalog dates within the past 24 months and exclude future dates. Store dates can differ from original film releases and include reissues, so they never become inferred film years or search constraints. Charts indicate interest in those storefronts, not worldwide popularity or a confirmed streaming offer.

`GET /api/scout/discover?mode=popular&region=ALL` supplies the suggestion shelf; `mode=recent` selects recent store releases. It requires no API key and consumes no web-search credits. Eight markets share an eight-second deadline with four concurrent requests. Popular and Recent reuse a 15-minute cache per region (three minutes for partial failures). Manual title search remains available when a chart fails.

Title searches also consult the full current chart cache for exact movie matches. This preserves the original Apple movie-page link when its keyword-search API misses a recent chart release. The linked page retains its actual storefront market, is labeled paid with its current offer unconfirmed, and does not acquire an invented price, original release year or playback claim.

**Worldwide** searches eight Apple storefronts (United States, United Kingdom, India, South Africa, Germany, France, Japan and Australia), alongside Internet Archive, Blender's public movie catalog, federated PeerTube search, Wikimedia Commons and configured global web discovery. Blender's public PeerTube search supplies movie candidates without an API key, independently of the wider PeerTube index; its metadata resolver must confirm public media before sampling. Storefront requests share a ten-second budget with at most four active requests. Choose a country preference to search its storefront instead. Each offer retains its market; a listing does not establish availability elsewhere. No URLs are rewritten into a local storefront, and no guessed movie IDs are generated.

Results show free, subscription, rental, purchase, paid or unconfirmed access. Catalog prices include their currency and market. A known paid service model (for example Netflix subscriptions) is labeled separately from a confirmed offer for this movie; YouTube videos and unfamiliar sites retain unconfirmed access. Evidence links open through the protected browser. Regional badges distinguish a catalog listing in the selected country, a supported service with the movie unconfirmed, an unknown region and a foreign-market offer. Check time and verification status distinguish listings, reachable pages and reachable media; a successful media probe is not a full playback test. Failed static reference streams are excluded. The server keeps no in-memory copy of search reports; repeat searches are answered from Atlas when a database is configured, as described under Phase 1 below.

Every **Open with ad blocking** action opens the source inside Vault. Required network interception and page protection must initialize successfully before navigation; failures appear in the interface. Sign-in, payment and regional requirements remain with the provider. Paid listings are never converted into preview streams or offered through the free-media relay. Ad blocking reduces advertising and tracking; effectiveness and playback compatibility vary by site.

Direct movie-page recognition includes current Disney+, Hulu, Peacock, HBO Max, Paramount+, Fandango at Home, Rakuten TV, Pluto TV and Filmzie routes. Subscription services are labeled paid; free service models are distinguished from evidence of an offer for this specific title. Mixed free/rental/purchase services retain unconfirmed access until primary movie evidence exists. A Plex guide page alone is excluded: up to three matching Plex movie pages receive a bounded primary-action check (3.5 seconds overall, 1 MiB per response). A visible movie-specific **Watch for Free** action can establish a page offer, while trailers, FAQs, recommendations, hidden content, external providers and guide-only pages cannot. It does not authorize a native playback sample without supported public media.

Free-media selections start with the protected relay enabled. A fatal HLS or native-player error remains visible and offers a retry with the current protection setting; errors never silently switch to a direct CDN connection. Each new search-result selection restores its requested protection setting.

### Media player and viewers

The console presents relayed media itself rather than through the browser's default controls. A relayed item is detected as video, audio, image or document from its URL and the scout's format label, and each kind opens in its own viewer on the same stage: a video player, an audio player, a zoomable image viewer and a PDF reader. Every item starts through the protected relay, and the stage shows which path its bytes take: **Protected relay · Shield on** or **Direct CDN · filtering off**. A failure keeps that path and offers **Retry with ad filtering**; the path only changes when the operator presses the filtering toggle for that item.

The video and audio players share one playback engine. HLS playlists go through hls.js; MP4, WebM and audio files use the browser's decoder; DASH manifests are reported as unsupported rather than attempted. The controls cover play, seek with buffered ranges and a hover time, 10-second skips, volume, playback speed, HLS quality with the active rendition shown, HLS audio tracks, captions, picture-in-picture, theater size and full screen. Captions list the subtitle tracks an HLS playlist declares or the WebVTT files a source publishes; the console does not discover subtitles yet. A statistics panel reports the engine, resolution, decoded and dropped frames, buffer ahead, the active rendition, hls.js's measured bandwidth and the last segment's load time, all read from the element and hls.js. Keyboard shortcuts follow the usual player conventions and are listed under the keyboard button. Volume, mute and speed persist in the browser.

Items selected in a session form a queue: the control bar steps between them, the queue panel lists and removes them, and **Play next when finished** advances automatically. The audio player draws a level meter from the relayed signal; it is only available through the relay, because routing a cross-origin element through the Web Audio graph would silence it. The image viewer zooms around the pointer, pans by drag, rotates, fits or shows actual pixels, and reports the image's real dimensions. The reader first asks the relay for the document's first byte and frames it for the browser's own PDF viewer only when the answer is a PDF; a provider's rate-limit or sign-in page is reported as the status it returned and never rendered. Other document formats are named as not yet rendered and offered to the protected browser instead. Media requests observed by a sandbox session can be tried in the player directly; they stay labelled as unverified observations.

The relay marks every response with `X-Content-Type-Options: nosniff`, and every response except a PDF with `Content-Security-Policy: sandbox`. Upstream status codes and content types still pass through, so a provider's error page or login wall is visible as such, but an HTML body renders as an inert opaque-origin document and can never run script in the console's origin. PDFs are exempt because Chrome's viewer refuses a sandboxed response or frame; the reader's check before framing covers them. The address bar accepts media URLs (`.m3u8`, `.mp4`, `.webm`, `.mp3`, `.ogg`, `.jpg`, `.png`, `.pdf` and similar) for the player and any other URL for the protected browser.

For broader discovery of websites beyond these catalogs, configure `TAVILY_API_KEY` or `BRAVE_SEARCH_API_KEY` as described below. Tavily is an alternative when Brave is unavailable. Without either key, the app shows that web discovery is unavailable while catalog search continues. Search snippets alone do not establish a free offer or local playback. Apple movie listings use its [Search API](https://performance-partners.apple.com/search-api) and require no API key. Provider failures and empty responses are visible under **Search coverage**.

Provider guidance was checked on 18 October 2026: [Netflix country coverage](https://help.netflix.com/en/node/14164), [Apple country features](https://support.apple.com/en-us/118205), [Prime Video country guidance](https://www.primevideo.com/help?nodeId=GD5REBNJD74BURF6), [Tubi markets](https://tubitv.com/help-center/About-Tubi/articles/4409969055259), and [Showmax's Tanzania closure](https://www.dstv.com/en-tz/watch/showmax/). These describe service policies rather than per-movie licensing, and should be refreshed as providers change.

### Automatic availability checks

Each movie search observes the server's outgoing country and checks up to six matching movie pages, alongside existing bounded free-media probes. In Worldwide mode, successful free-video playback samples rank first regardless of the check location, followed by media responses and matching movie-page responses. Explicit country preferences apply local evidence only when its check country matches. Storefront markets remain visible. Missing links, explicit country restrictions and failed or blocked checks rank lower. Cards show the checked URL, observation time and actual check country. One network observation never confirms every country.

Page checks distinguish a matching reachable movie page, a regional/legal restriction, a missing link, sign-in, a restricted automated check and an inconclusive response. HTTP 403 alone is not a country block, and HTTP 200 is not confirmed playback. The parser ignores scripts, hidden content, navigation, recommendations, trailers and FAQs when looking for movie access messages. It checks movie identity and release year, and does not treat a sign-in redirect or a soft 404 as a working movie page. A reachable media response means its sampled format/signature was recognized; it does not establish complete playback, licensing or a paid user's entitlement.

Checks use the same direct server connection for the country observation and movie/media requests. The [Cloudflare diagnostic endpoint](https://developers.cloudflare.com/fundamentals/reference/cdn-cgi-endpoint/) is reduced to its country code; IP addresses and raw trace data are not kept or returned. Positive country observations are reused for up to 60 seconds. Page responses are limited to 512 KiB and five seconds, with at most five public-web redirects. Private/local destinations and nonstandard ports are rejected, including DNS and redirect targets. Page checks do not execute provider scripts or submit credentials. The complete search has a 44-second deadline; links outside its check budget remain explicitly unchecked. Catalog-only cached reports retain their original check timestamps.

### Free playback samples

Up to two supported, reachable free videos are sampled automatically in parallel, preferring different sites and avoiding repeated tests of an identical media URL. Free-media discovery resolves relevant Internet Archive item pages, public PeerTube video metadata and supported public-domain movie pages into their explicit media sources. Metadata must identify the requested movie, and failed resolutions remain listings. This enrichment checks at most six targets with three workers, four seconds per target and a six-second overall budget. Search snippets do not authorize playback of paid or unknown sources.

For equally relevant and equally reachable candidates, explicit browser-compatible format metadata and lower known average bitrates help choose which copies fit the short sample budget. These scheduling hints never confirm playback or affect country availability; successful native decoding still determines the playback badge.

Supported discovery routes include Internet Archive `/details/` and `/embed/` item pages, public PeerTube `/w/`, `/videos/watch/` and `/videos/embed/` links, and individual [Public Domain Movies](https://publicdomainmovie.net/) pages. Public Domain Movies links are checked against the exact Archive item in the page's download section; this adds discovery pages while retaining the original media provider. PeerTube supports public files and HLS playlists, including a public CDN explicitly identified by the hosting instance's video API. Other websites retain their page-access checks and protected browsing while playback support remains unconfirmed.

Feature discovery also searches [PublicDomainMovie.org](https://publicdomainmovie.org/) and [ArcoirisTV](https://www.arcoiris.tv/) through their public search forms. The former must embed one matching Archive feature in its primary movie content; the latter must publish matching first-party video metadata and a feature-length runtime. These searches require no account or API key. Public Domain Movies' feature browse adds exact title/year matches from its advertised catalog pages. Archive search considers up to 30 listings, resolves at most six relevant items within eight seconds, rejects mismatched filename years and ancillary videos, and prefers known streaming derivatives and explicit H.264/WebM metadata before other encodings. Archive documents its [optimized 512kb MP4 derivative](https://blog.archive.org/2010/04/26/better-mp4-h-264-derivatives-at-archive-org/); these format hints help choose a candidate, while decoded frame progress is still required to confirm a start.

Each sample uses a fresh muted Chromium profile and an ITEMBA-owned local video player; provider pages, ads, logins and payment flows are not executed. Media is relayed through the same guarded server connection as the country observation. Chromium can request only that sample's local player and media resources. MP4/WebM and supported HLS streams use at most 6 MiB of upstream data per sample and a 12-second browser deadline inside a 14-second sample budget. HLS uses the locally built `playback-hls.min.js` asset and selects the lowest advertised bandwidth.

Pluto TV and Filmzie movie links also receive bounded **provider access checks**, declared by the `pluto-tv` and `filmzie` drivers, both when discovered by title and when pasted directly into search. Up to four links share a six-second budget with two workers, covering both sites before additional copies. Pluto's explicit location-unavailable page and Filmzie's public main-video country response are recorded as restrictions, with the actual check location shown. These checks do not count as decoded playback samples. An accessible Pluto page remains unconfirmed until its main movie content can be attributed safely; no private or guessed stream API is used.

Filmzie metadata must identify a matching feature title, year and full movie asset, followed by a public guest playback response. Trailers, DRM, sign-in requirements and explicit country restrictions stop the check. An approved main movie file may enter the existing protected native sampler; provider stream URLs stay private and are not exposed as generic client relay links. Filmzie's server-inserted-ad HLS remains unconfirmed because a preroll cannot establish that the movie started. The live checks on 18 October 2026 found both providers restricted from the server connection in Tanzania; Worldwide remains the search default.

The sampler applies Shield rules to its initial media, redirects and every HLS playlist, key, map and segment. Explicit ad cues and DRM playlists stop verification without rewriting the movie timeline or retrying without protection. Blocked domains and explicit custom URL rules apply even when an address ends in a movie-file extension. A protection or content-attribution failure cannot receive a successful playback badge or ranking boost.

Additional supported free providers are **Fawesome**, **NASA+**, **NFB / ONF**, and the **Catsuka player explicitly authorized by filmmaker Nina Paley**. Their public movie metadata must match the title, year where supplied, feature runtime and exact main asset. Guest restrictions, paid offers, trailers, DRM and unattributed advertising remain unverified. Signed provider media stays private; movie pages open through Vault with ad filtering enabled. Fawesome follows its normal player's preference for an explicitly supplied, movie-node-bound main HLS source, with the public main MP4 contract supported when no HLS source is declared. Both stay behind the same protected native checker; no stream path is guessed, no oversized MP4 is downloaded and no unprotected retry is made. The creator adapter follows the filmmaker's explicit publisher link. NFB's exact public embed supplies its unchanged signed movie stream; NASA+ publishes its main video separately from preview media.

The global query showed that free result first alongside a paid storefront offer. These are measured starts, not confirmation of uninterrupted complete-film playback or availability from other networks. Large MP4 headers can consume the bounded sample budget; an explicitly supplied main HLS source can use the provider's ordinary segmented playback path without increasing the sample limits.

NASA+ and NFB add public catalog searches without API keys or extra web-search credits. It does not rely on a fixed set of example films. Popular and new feeds share an eight-second deadline, with at most four or five public requests on a cold start, respectively. ### Enable Tavily web discovery

1. Open [Tavily](https://app.tavily.com), create an account or sign in, and obtain an API key from the dashboard. Its [free plan](https://www.tavily.com/pricing) currently includes 1,000 credits per month without a credit card.
2. Save the key as `TAVILY_API_KEY=your_key_here` in the project-root `.env.local`. Keep keys out of chat, source code and browser configuration.
3. Restart `itemba.exe` from the project root. Existing process environment variables take precedence over the local file. The file accepts only `PORT`, `TAVILY_API_KEY`, `BRAVE_SEARCH_API_KEY` and `ITEMBA_DATABASE_URL`.
4. Search for a movie and open **Search coverage**. A working connection shows **Tavily web search** with the number of destination links returned. Authentication and quota failures appear without exposing the key.

The app requests [basic search](https://docs.tavily.com/documentation/api-reference/endpoint/search), with automatic search-depth selection, generated answers and raw-page extraction disabled. Basic requests cost one credit. Worldwide searches omit country and preferred-domain fields and search for free, rental and purchase options across the web. Guide/reference domains are excluded upstream and all returned destinations are filtered again locally. Explicit country preferences may add country and service hints; they never guarantee local title availability. Tavily has a bounded ten-second request budget inside the 44-second search deadline, which also covers free-source discovery, availability and playback checks.

When both keys are configured, Tavily takes precedence. The app sends one web-provider request per search; it does not automatically retry a failed Tavily request through Brave. This avoids unexpected requests to a second provider. Configure only the provider you intend to use.

### Optional Brave web discovery

1. Open the [Brave Search API dashboard](https://api-dashboard.search.brave.com/app/keys), sign in or register, activate a **Search** plan, and create a key. Review billing and usage limits in your account. [Brave's setup guide](https://api-dashboard.search.brave.com/documentation/quickstart) describes the account and plan steps.
2. Open `.env.local` in the project root and set `BRAVE_SEARCH_API_KEY=your_key_here`. A blank local file is supplied for this workspace; `.env.example` is the reusable template. Keep the actual key in `.env.local`, which is excluded from version control. The key belongs to the Go server and must never use a `VITE_` prefix or appear in browser code.
3. Restart `itemba.exe` from the project root. Environment variables already set for the process take precedence over `.env.local`, including an explicitly blank variable. The optional file supports `BRAVE_SEARCH_API_KEY`, `TAVILY_API_KEY`, `PORT` and `ITEMBA_DATABASE_URL`; quoted literal values and comments are accepted, without shell expansion.
4. Search for a movie and expand **Search coverage**. A working connection shows **Brave web search** with the number of destination links returned. Invalid keys or plans show an authentication error; rate limits show a retry or quota message. An authenticated search can legitimately return no matches.

Brave searches the worldwide index (`country=ALL`); a selected country supplies a location hint. Recognized movie-site page titles are normalized for matching, while the original title is preserved in the description. Web listings remain unverified with unknown access until there is evidence of price or availability. Opening a result uses the same protected browser as catalog results.

Reports are not kept in memory between requests. When a database is configured, web-discovery listings are recorded in Atlas like any other location, so the retention terms of a configured web-search provider are the operator's to observe. See [Brave's retention guidance](https://api-dashboard.search.brave.com/documentation/resources/help-feedback) for its current standard terms. Setup and connection errors never include key values.

## Quick Start

### 1. Run Unified Platform (Local Hybrid Mode — Zero Docker Required)

The built frontend (`web/dist`) is served directly by `itemba` on `http://127.0.0.1:8080`:

```powershell
# Start the ITEMBA NET Control Plane + Dashboard on http://127.0.0.1:8080
./itemba.exe
```

The binary takes a `--role` flag: `console`, `crawler`, `probe` or `all` (the default). Only `console` and `all` run in this phase; `crawler` and `probe` refuse to start until Phases 2 and 4 of the [Implementation Plan](IMPLEMENTATION-PLAN.md) deliver their workers. `--config` names the optional local configuration file (default `.env.local`), `--static` the built dashboard directory (default `web/dist`) and `--database` the Atlas PostgreSQL URL (default from `ITEMBA_DATABASE_URL`).

### Phase 1: memory and generality

Phase 1 is under way. Atlas holds the core schema from the white paper, objects, aliases, hosts, locations and evidence, as migration `0002_atlas_core.sql`, applied automatically at startup. Lens (`internal/lens`) parses a search into a typed object query (type, title and year, or a URL or hash), normalises titles so the same film matches itself across spellings, places scout's checks on the verification ladder, and ranks candidates under the hundred cap with host diversity.

Every search writes back to Atlas when a database is configured. Each source becomes a location on its host with evidence at the level its checks earn: listed, reachable, identified or rendered, with method, status, egress country and observation time. A source whose title matches the query joins the query's object; any other title gets its own object, because a location belongs to what it says it holds. Pruned sources are not locations and are skipped.

`GET /api/scout/search/stream?query=Sintel&region=ALL&fullLengthOnly=true` is the read-through search, as server-sent events:

- `known`: what Atlas already holds for the object, ranked, with effective levels after freshness decay. It arrives immediately.
- `report`: the fresh discovery burst, in the same shape as `POST /api/scout/search`, plus `atlas` with what was written.
- `merged`: the ranked hundred across known and fresh locations, each with its level and name, observation time, egress and origin (`index`, `fresh` or `both`).
- `done`, with the total duration.

`POST /api/scout/search` keeps its shape and gains an `atlas` summary when a database is configured. Freshness decay: rendered evidence keeps its level for 24 hours, identified for three days and reachable for seven, then drops one step until the location is probed again. Level 4, clean, is not yet awarded, because ad exposure is measured in Phase 5.

**A local database without Docker.** PostgreSQL 17 with pgvector runs inside the Debian WSL2 distro, which is the execution host anyway:

### Phase 2: the crawler

Phase 2 is under way. Discovery stops being a set of hand-written providers and becomes a crawl that runs without an operator typing queries.

**Drivers as data.** `drivers/*.yaml` describe how to search a host and read its listings: the search URL and parameters with `{query}`, `{query_phrase}` and `{market}` placeholders, the storefront markets to cover, the JSON path to the items (a bare array, an object read as its values, or `.` for the document itself), fields an item must or must not have (`require`, `forbid`, `require_min`, `min_duration_seconds`), field mappings with transforms (`year_of_date`, `year_in_text`, `duration_from_millis`, `duration_from_seconds`, `duration_seconds`, `html_unescape`, `html_text`), prefix and suffix stripping, truncation, templated defaults, storefront offers, constants, a status name, a timeout and rate limits. Unknown keys and invalid rules are refused when a driver loads, and one broken file fails the whole load. Seven drivers ship, one per JSON catalog the search used to reach through hand-written code: `apple-movies` (eight storefronts), `internet-archive`, `peertube-sepia`, `peertube-blender`, `wikimedia-commons`, `nasa-plus` and `nfb`. Each is held to the legacy provider's own test fixture in `internal/crawler`, so parity is proven rather than assumed. The binary loads drivers from `--drivers` (default `drivers`) in every role, so a driver fix needs no rebuild.

**The search runs through the drivers.** The console's discovery burst fans out to the drivers (storefront drivers once per market, sharing a ten-second deadline and four slots worldwide, or to the chosen country alone) and then applies the pipeline's own rules: relevance where a driver asks for it, the Archive edition scoring and metadata resolution, and everything downstream from availability checks to playback samples. The hand-written search functions for those seven catalogs are deleted, along with their types and direct tests; their fixtures live on as driver parity tests. The HTML catalogs (Public Domain Movies, PublicDomainMovie.org, ArcoirisTV) and Fawesome's guest search stay hand-written until HTML drivers exist. Atlas records neutral observations now, with the conversion from search reports living in the console, so Atlas no longer depends on scout.

**Frontier and scheduler.** `atlas.frontier` (migration 0003) holds every URL the crawler owes a fetch: a driver search for an object in a market, or a page fetch for a location whose evidence has gone stale. Rows are leased with `FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED`, so several crawler processes can share one frontier. A per-host token bucket and concurrency cap come from each driver's `rate`.

**Fetch tier one.** Plain HTTP with rotating identity profiles, a 2 MiB body limit and per-host exponential backoff on 403, 429 and 503 up to six hours. Tiers two and three, headless rendering and a Vault session, come later in Phase 2.

**The crawler role.** `--role crawler`, or `all` with a database, runs the loop: every five minutes it seeds a search per driver and market for the objects Atlas knows and a page fetch for stale locations; every thirty seconds it leases a batch and processes it with four workers. Searches record listings as locations with `driver:<name>` listed evidence; page fetches record reachable or listed evidence with the HTTP status. A completed search rests for 24 hours, a page for 72, and failures back off by attempt. `GET /api/crawler/status` reports the drivers, cycles, the last cycle, frontier counts and hosts in backoff, and metrics gain `itemba_crawler_fetches_total` and `itemba_crawler_listings_total`. The crawler role refuses to start without a database.

Verified on 18 October 2026 against the WSL2 database: one cycle for a seeded title enqueued eight Apple searches, one Archive search and one stale page, processed all ten, recorded ten listings and one reachable page, and left nothing due until the refresh windows; the listings read back through the index with their driver evidence.

Verified on 18 October 2026: a worldwide *Sintel* search from the binary reported every driver by its status name, resolved the Archive listings into a playable source and a reachable page, and took the same seventeen seconds as before the conversion.

**HTML drivers.** A driver may answer in HTML: `response: html`, `items` is a CSS selector for the listing elements, `exclude` drops items inside navigation, footers, scripts or hidden elements, `within` requires an ancestor such as the catalog container, and `pages` follows a bounded set of further result pages linked from the first. HTML fields are read with `select` relative to the item (or to its `closest` ancestor), `attr` for an attribute or the element text otherwise, with `href` and `src` resolved against the page. A `same_url` rule refuses a response that came from a different page, so a host cannot substitute its homepage for its catalog. Selectors come from the cascadia library over the standard HTML parser. Three HTML drivers replace the hand-written walkers: `public-domain-movies` (the browse catalog and its two advertised continuation pages, with the card's `/year?v=` link as the release year), `arcoiris-tv` and `publicdomainmovie-org` (the observed search forms). Their `require_relevance: exact` rule keeps only the listing whose canonical title names the requested feature, with a known year agreeing and a requested year carried onto the resolver; `keep` caps what survives.

**Steps.** A host that needs configuration or a guest token before it will answer declares `steps`: ordered requests whose `capture` entries read variables by CSS selector, JSON path or regular expression, with `unique` requiring exactly one candidate, `match` filtering them, `require_text` confirming the response still carries the contract, `assert` comparing captured values, `same_url` and `no_redirect` keeping the request on its origin, and `cache_seconds` keeping public configuration between searches while a token is fetched fresh every time. The `fawesome` driver is the first: the shell names the current player bundle, the bundle yields the public identifiers under the same checks the legacy code made, the guest endpoint hands out an anonymous token, and the search sends it in a same-origin header. Its parity test reproduces the legacy flow exactly: four requests on a cold search, six after a second. A cold search is four sequential round trips including the player bundle, which once outran the legacy eight-second budget inside a loaded burst during testing, so the driver's budget is twelve seconds; later searches reuse the cached configuration. 

**Feeds.** A driver may declare `feeds`: catalogs the host publishes without a query, which reuse the search's steps, endpoint, headers, response format and item rules and change only what differs. A feed may add `steps` of its own, take its parameters from a URL the host handed out under `params_from` (an allowlist pattern per key, identifiers that are dropped and replaced by the driver's fresh ones, keys that must be present), set a `limit` and `timeout_seconds`, and add `fields`, such as a `catalog_date` read with the new `date:<layout>` transform. JSON paths gained `[key=value]` filters, so `channels[name=New Arrivals].url` is the URL of the one row the site names, and a capture that finds several candidates is ambiguous under `unique`. The `fawesome` driver's `popular` and `recent` feeds replace the hand-written shelf client: popular is the site's `getPopularSearchURL` contract, recent follows the New Arrivals row of `sub-categories.php` with only the catalog controls it was observed to carry, and both take a fresh guest token per feed over the cached public configuration (four requests cold for popular, five for recent, two fewer warm). The shelf keeps its fifteen-minute cache, its region echo and its rule that a film without a known feature runtime is not advertised. The legacy guest client, its catalog parsing and their tests are deleted; the driver's parity tests reproduce the legacy tests, every refused feed URL included.

**Access checks.** A driver may declare `check` instead of, or beside, `search`: a regular expression for the host's movie pages, an optional `url_title` for the slug of a pasted link, and ordered `requests`, each with its own timeout, byte budget and redirect policy (`public`, `same_path` or `none`), soft captures that leave a missing variable absent, and `rules` judged in order, where a `when` fires if every test holds and an `unless` fires if one fails. Tests cover the HTTP status, the final URL, captured variables (patterns in which `{name}` is a variable's quoted value), numeric ranges, and the judgments only the pipeline can make: `page` facts read from an HTML answer (a bot challenge, a sign-in wall, a soft 404, whether the page names the listing's film; what counts as visible content is one rule now, shared with `visible: true` captures) and `metadata`, whether the title, year and duration the host declared name the film. A result is a preflight status and reason with a detail, or a `bind` of the main media the host handed over, which only the protected sampler may use. The `pluto-tv` and `filmzie` drivers replace the hand-written inspectors with the same verdicts: Pluto's first-party location refusal read only from a heading a visitor sees, its sign-in and challenge pages, and a reachable page that still proves no playback; Filmzie's page state, its one main asset, the guest stream response with its country, account, trailer and DRM answers, the explicit legacy movie file that may be sampled and the server-inserted-ad stream that may not. Captures gained `paths`, `transform`, `json` (keep an object as JSON text, or decode the JSON a script assigns) and reading a JSON variable by path; the fetcher gained bounded redirects (https and public hosts only, optionally the same path and hosts) and strict body limits. A check-only driver has no search: the crawler seeds nothing for it and the search burst skips it. Thirteen drivers ship. The legacy Pluto TV and Filmzie code and tests are deleted; their tests live on as driver parity tests in `internal/crawler` and as pipeline tests in `internal/scout`. NASA+, NFB, Fawesome's movie page and the creator catalog keep their Go playback inspectors, which bind a page to its media for the sampler, and the Archive metadata resolver remains. Verified on 19 October 2026 from this connection: the popular feed returned twenty full-length listings in four public requests and new arrivals nineteen in five, each with its catalog date; the Pluto TV check read the service's location refusal at `/intl/` and the Filmzie check its country answer from the guest stream response, both recorded as region restrictions and neither as playback.

**Runtime rules added for these hosts.** `require_empty` and `require_match` (a regular expression with the item's own values substituted) for JSON items, `require_document` for top-level checks, `reject` and `max_length` and `range` on fields, `optional` to blank a field that fails its rules instead of dropping the item, the `url_query` transform, a `strip_article` template filter, and an opt-in live check: `ITEMBA_LIVE_DRIVER=<name> go test ./internal/crawler -run TestLiveDriverSearch` runs one shipped driver against its host and logs what it listed or the exact failure.

Verified on 19 October 2026 from the binary: a worldwide *Night of the Living Dead* search resolved ArcoirisTV's and PublicDomainMovie.org's pages into free sources alongside the Archive copies, and *The Shape of Shadows* came back from Fawesome's guest search with a decoded start on both a cold and a cached run. The hand-written searches for the three HTML catalogs and for Fawesome are deleted with their direct tests; the shared Fawesome guest client, the catalog resolvers and the playback inspectors remain.

Still open in Phase 2: fetch tiers two and three, the 72-hour unattended run, and egress rotation.

### Phase 3: domains

Phase 3 is under way. Audio was the first new domain; pictures and documents have followed with the same four pieces each, identity in Lens, a probe, a Vault presentation and a first set of drivers.

**Audio identity in Lens.** A recording is what its tags say it is: a title, a performer and a duration (`internal/lens/audio.go`). `ParseAs(query, kind)` reads the search form's kind over any type word in the query and, for audio, a performer phrased as *Kind of Blue by Miles Davis* or *Miles Davis - Kind of Blue* into `Creator`. The by-form is read at its last "by", and refused when the words after it read as part of the title (*Blinded by the Light*) or when both sides are one word (*Killed by Death*). `AudioListing` reads a host's tags the same way, a performer tag winning over one written into the title. `SameRecording` and `DurationsAgree` say when two listings are one recording: the same title, performers that agree when both are known, durations within three seconds or two percent when both are known. `AudioRelevance` scores a listing against a query: a title that merely shares some words scores nothing, a listing whose performer is unknown scores lower when the query names one, and a different performer scores zero.

**Atlas.** Migration `0004_audio_identity.sql` gives objects a `creator` and `creator_key` and locations a `duration_seconds`, and widens the identity constraint to type, title key, creator key and year. The creator key is empty for every object recorded so far, so a video object still conflicts on type, title key and year alone, and the upsert names the constraint rather than its columns. A listing joins the asked recording when its title matches and its performer is unknown or agrees with the one the query names; every other credited recording of that title is an object of its own, and a lookup that names a performer leaves the other performers' recordings out. Listings carry a `kind` and a `creator`, so the crawler records a driver's listings under the driver's kind.

**Drivers of a kind.** A driver declares `kind` (default `video`); the search fans a query out to the drivers of its kind only, and the crawler seeds a driver only with objects of its kind. `drivers/internet-archive-audio.yaml` is the first audio driver, the Advanced Search over `mediatype:audio`, with `creator` read as the first of several credits. The search index names no length, so the search pipeline reads the item metadata of the kept listings, six per search: an item that is audio, public and not dark gives its first file in a container the console plays, the summed length of its tracks in that container, and its performer when the listing had none.

**The audio search.** `SearchOptions.Kind` and the search routes' `kind` (`video`, the default, `audio`, `image` or `document`) choose the pipeline. An audio search keeps the listings whose tags answer the query, resolves the Archive items, checks each file for reach with the media probe, which now recognises MP3, FLAC and WAVE bytes beside video, and checks the pages of the rest within a budget of eight. It never prunes for length. The report carries `kind`, every source its `kind`, `creator` and `tracks`, and the ranked hundred its performer.

**The audio sample.** Up to two free recordings whose files answered as audio get a muted beginning sample, the way two free movies do: the same throwaway Chromium with every request intercepted, the same local harness relaying the file through Shield, the same slots and the same 14-second window. The harness serves an `<audio>` page for a recording (`audioPlaybackHTML`), the sampler reads the audio element and judges time advancing at normal speed over three readings and at least a second, with no frames or picture to demand, and the first track is judged by its own listed length, not the item's. The result is a `PlaybackCheck` with method `isolated_audio_sample`; a started, confirmed sample is rendered on the ladder, in the merged list and in the evidence Atlas is given, exactly as a movie sample is. Only an Archive audio listing whose file sits under the item its metadata evidence names, in a container the console plays, is eligible (`eligibleAudioPlayback`); every other free recording says why it was not sampled.

**Tracks and the queue.** The Archive resolver carries the item's tracks in order, with their lengths and container. A recording card lists them, each playable from where it stands, and playing the recording queues every track behind the chosen one in the media stage's session queue, which already advances to the next item when one ends. The audio player warms the next queued track's beginning, muted, while the current one plays, so the switch serves from the browser's media cache: near-gapless, not sample-accurate.

**The Console.** The search form offers Movies or Audio. In audio, the query field asks for the title with the performer, the full-length preference, the discovery shelf and the free-catalog examples step aside, and the results are recording cards: performer, year, listed length and container, the sample's outcome in the recording's own words, the reach of the file or page, the track list, the page in the protected browser and, for a free file that answered as audio, playback through the relay in the media stage's audio player.

Verified on 19 October 2026 against a separate `itemba_phase3` database on the WSL2 cluster: *Kind of Blue by Miles Davis* returned five recordings in six seconds, every file reachable, the first two the 1959 album as six MP3 and five AAC tracks with their summed lengths; *Kind of Blue* alone returned 21 recordings under 20 objects, one per credited recording; and a worldwide *Sintel* search on this binary returned the same two sources at the same levels as the binary built before this slice. A race surfaced on the way: Lens normalised titles through one shared `transform.Chain`, which carries buffers of its own, and the audio pipeline is the first to normalise from several goroutines at once; the chain is built per call now.

Verified on 20 October 2026 against the same database: *Kind of Blue by Miles Davis* sampled two recordings in a 17-second search, both started (an AAC first track advanced 1.2 seconds against its listed 547-second length, an MP3 one a second), both shown at rendered in the ranked list, and Atlas received seven evidence rows for five locations; in the dashboard, playing the twelve-track edition queued all twelve tracks in order and the first was 20 seconds in through the relay. On an earlier run one of the two samples timed out against a slow file and was reported as not started.

**The acoustic fingerprint.** The audio sample now takes a fingerprint of what it hears (`internal/lens/fingerprint.go`, type `acoustic-bands-v1`). Beside the playing element, the harness page fetches the first 640 KiB of the same relayed file, decodes them offline at 11025 Hz mono with the Web Audio API and reduces up to thirty seconds to one 32-bit word every 93 ms: Hann windows of 4096 samples every 1024, 33 bands spaced logarithmically from 300 Hz to 2000 Hz, and bit *m* set when the energy difference between bands *m* and *m*+1 grew since the previous window, the band-energy scheme of Haitsma and Kalker. The same steps run in Go as `AcousticFingerprint`, the reference the page's output is checked against and the generator of fixtures; the band table is a shared literal so no rounding can move an edge. Masters of one recording run a few percent apart (the first side of *Kind of Blue* is the famous case), and a two percent difference both drifts the frames six apart over thirty seconds and moves every band's energy a third of a band, which no comparison can undo afterwards, so the page also fingerprints the beginning resampled at one, two and three percent either way: a family of six that travels with the natural fingerprint. Two fingerprints are compared by the bit error rate over their overlap at the best alignment within three seconds, trying the other side's family; at or under 0.35 they are the same sound, and unrelated sound sits near 0.5. The fingerprint rides on the `PlaybackCheck` as `fingerprint` with a `fingerprintStatus`; a confirmed sample waits up to four seconds for one still pending; a movie sample carries none; and AAC in an MP4 whose index trails the file decodes only in the player, which the status says.

**The fingerprint library.** Migration `0005_fingerprints.sql` adds `atlas.fingerprints`, one row per location and type: the length, the span covered, the sampled file's length, the words, the family, when it was observed, and what the library concluded on arrival (the nearest stored fingerprint, its distance, the verdict and its effect), so a lookup repeats the verdict without comparing again. A later arrival revises that verdict where it is a better answer: every stored fingerprint the new one is the same as takes it as its peer, confirmed when the two now sit in one object, unless it already has a nearer same peer; one that had nothing to compare with takes the arrival as its nearest; a nearer different fingerprint changes nothing, since it says nothing new about identity. The revised verdicts travel back with the recording summary, so the cards of the search that caused them show them at once, and the order in which locations were recorded does not show in what the library says of them. Objects gain a `content_key`, empty for every object recorded so far and widened into the identity constraint as 0004 did for the creator key. The console's evidence bridge hands a sample's fingerprint to Atlas with its listing, and `RecordObservations` compares it in Go with the stored fingerprints of its type (the most recent four thousand; an alignment search is nothing an index can shift, so the bytes stay generic) and lets the sound decide the object. The nearest same sound on a file of a compatible length, within ten seconds or five percent once scaled to the speed the sounds matched at, names the object the listing joins: *confirmed* when that is the object its tags name, *joined* when it is another. When the object its tags name has been heard and sounds different, or sounds the same only at the start on a file of another length, the listing is *kept apart* as an object of its own under a content key derived from the fingerprint. Otherwise it stays under its tags, and the tag candidate is stored only then, so a title the sound overruled leaves no empty object behind. A lookup carries each location's verdict as `identity`; the ranked list and the recording cards show it as Confirmed, Matched or Separated by fingerprint, with the peer and the measure, and the recording card says how the fingerprint went.

**Identity in Lens.** `MatchRecordings`, which `SameRecording` now rests on, lets the sound overrule the tags when both identities carry a fingerprint: the same sound is the same recording whatever the tags say, a different sound is a different recording even under the same tags, and the same beginning on files whose lengths disagree as editions is an edit. The audio pipeline compares the fingerprints its two samples computed and says on each source what the other sounded like; a source joined by sound takes the better relevance of the two, so a listing that merely contains the title ranks with the recording it is.

**The fixture set.** `internal/lens/testdata/fingerprints.json` holds thirteen recordings with known fingerprints and twenty known pairs: eight synthetic recordings generated from the unit tests' tunes as editions would be published (a lead-in, another level, noise, cut short, a slow master, a radio edit on a shorter file, a different take under the same tags) and five sampled from the Internet Archive through the same decoding the harness uses, three editions of *So What* (two MP3 uploads of one master and an AAC edition whose master runs two percent apart) and two other tracks of the album. `TestFixtureFingerprintsConfirmTheirKnownPairs` is the Phase 3 exit check for the library: the fingerprints confirm or deny identity on all twenty pairs, where the criterion asks nine in ten. The two MP3 editions match at 0.275, the AAC edition at 0.242 against one and 0.249 against the other by the family, and every pair of different tracks sits between 0.46 and 0.48. Without the family the AAC edition measured 0.48 against both, which is what the first live run reported; the diagnosis, that true resampling by two percent brought it to 0.24 while stretching the frame sequence did not, is why the family is taken at capture time, where the audio is still in hand.

**Pictures.** The second domain follows the audio structure. A picture is what its tags say, a title, a maker and a year, until something has seen it; what it looks like is a perceptual hash (`internal/lens/image.go`, type `image-dhash-v1`): the picture in grey, box-sampled to a grid of seventeen by sixteen cells, and a bit set wherever a cell is brighter than its right neighbour, 256 bits in eight words that the fingerprint library stores like the acoustic ones. Two hashes are compared by the share of differing bits with no alignment to search; at or under 0.12 they are the same picture, and unrelated pictures sit near 0.5. `ParseAs` reads a maker out of a picture query as it reads a performer, *Starry Night by Van Gogh*. A picture the host's search returned keeps at least the host's own match (`HostMatchRelevance`, 0.25), because a file name rarely says what a picture shows. `drivers/wikimedia-commons-images.yaml` is the first image driver: the Commons search over bitmap files with the original, a 640-pixel thumbnail, the dimensions, the credited artist, the licence (a new `license` listing field) and the description from the file page's structured metadata; the file URLs lose their tracking parameters through the new `strip_query` transform.

**The picture probe.** No browser is needed to prove a file is a picture. The pipeline (`internal/scout/image.go`) fetches the first megabyte of the original by a range request and decodes its header for the dimensions and format (JPEG, PNG and GIF; a WebP or TIFF answers as unsupported and still opens in the viewer), then decodes the thumbnail, or a small original, whole for the hash. A decoded file is identified on the ladder; no sandbox render runs yet, so no picture is rendered. The probe takes at most eight files a search, two at a time and a quarter of a second apart, because Wikimedia's upload host answers a burst of a couple of dozen requests with HTTP 429, which the probe then says. The result is an `ImageCheck` on the source; the console's evidence bridge records it as `image_decode` evidence with the dimensions and hands the hash to the fingerprint library, whose comparison and verdicts are the same routine as for recordings, with the identity method `perceptual_hash` and the detail in the picture's words. The movie page check learnt that a file page names its picture as "File:Name.jpg". The dashboard gains a Pictures mode: picture cards with the thumbnail, maker, year, dimensions, format and licence, what the probe decoded, the hash verdict, and the picture opened in the zoomable viewer through the relay.

Verified on 20 October 2026 against the same database: *Starry Night by Van Gogh* probed eight of twelve Commons uploads in fifteen seconds, decoded every header (from 1879×1500 to the 44567×35291 Google Art Project scan, whose header sits in its first megabyte) and stored eight hashes; the index joined five uploads of the painting under different file names as one picture at distances from 0.00 to 0.06 and confirmed one against another, while the two Google Art Project scans, framed differently, stayed apart at 0.17 and above, which a global hash cannot bridge. *Moon landing* decoded nine of twelve files with their dimensions and told every photograph apart at 0.30 and more. **Documents.** The third domain follows the same structure. A document is what its tags say, a title, an author, a year and a page count, until something has read it; what it says is a text fingerprint (`internal/lens/document.go`, type `text-simhash-v2`): a similarity hash of the body of the text, in which every word and every pair of neighbouring words is hashed to 256 bits, weighted by how often it occurs, and each bit of the result is set when more features voted one than zero, so texts that share most of their words share most of their bits. The body is found first (`TextBody`), because a scanned book opens with endpapers whose OCR is noise, a publisher's catalogue, a title page and a table of contents, and two scans of one work differ most there: the words are read in windows of a hundred, a window reads as prose when at least 55 in a hundred are words of three or more letters, at most 25 are in capitals and at most 10 carry a digit, and the body begins at the first three such windows in a row; 6,000 words from there are hashed, and a text with no such run, such as a scan whose OCR is noise throughout, gets no fingerprint rather than one that matches nothing. Two fingerprints are compared by the share of differing bits, like the picture hash; at or under 0.15 they are the same text. Weighting features by their count is what makes the hash survive OCR noise and the differences between editions: on Archive scans of one work the distances sit between 0.02 and 0.11, with a retelling at 0.13, while unrelated English prose sits near 0.25 rather than 0.5, since every English text shares its function words; the alternatives measured on the same scans (each distinct feature voting once, capped or logarithmic counts, pairs alone) widen that gap but push the same work's editions to 0.2 and beyond, so the count stays. A catalogue credit is read as a name, so "Carroll, Lewis, 1832-1898" is Lewis Carroll (`NormalizeAuthor`); `ParseAs` reads an author out of a document query as it reads a performer; a listing the host returned keeps at least the host's own match. `drivers/internet-archive-texts.yaml` is the first document driver: the Archive's advanced search over the texts media type with the title, the primary credit, the year, the collections and the page count the host states (a new `pages` listing field). The files come from the item metadata, which the search reads for the kept listings, preferring the Text PDF to its grayscale derivatives and naming the OCR text beside it.

**The document probe.** No library and no browser are needed to prove a file is a PDF. The pipeline (`internal/scout/document.go` and `pdf.go`) fetches the first 512 KiB and the last 256 KiB of the file by range requests, following the Archive's redirect to the host that holds the item, confirms the header, reads the page count from the linearisation dictionary when the file has one or from the page tree's root otherwise, and says whether that count is the file's own or the host's. The text comes from the OCR text beside a scan when the item has one, because a scan's own text layer is often thin or absent, and from the PDF's Flate content streams otherwise; a plain text file is read as it is, and EPUB, DjVu and MOBI answer as unsupported. A parsed file is identified on the ladder; no sandbox render runs yet, so no document is rendered. The probe takes at most six files a search, two at a time, with the picture probe's pacing. The result is a `DocumentCheck` on the source; the console's evidence bridge records it as `document_parse` evidence with the format and the page count and hands the fingerprint to the fingerprint library, with the identity method `text_fingerprint` and the detail in the document's words. The dashboard gains a Documents mode: document cards with the thumbnail, author, year, page count and collection, what the probe read, the fingerprint verdict, and the PDF opened in the reader through the relay, which checks the relay's answer again before framing it.

**The video fingerprint.** The original domain gets its fingerprint last, from the sample it already takes. A video is what its tags say until something has watched it; what it shows is the sampled frame sequence (`internal/lens/video.go`, type `video-frames-v1`): the harness page draws the playing video onto a canvas of seventeen by sixteen cells every half second of media time and hands the sampler each frame's grey cells, the sampler hashes each frame as a picture is hashed, and the sequence of frame hashes, in order, is the fingerprint, at most sixty frames over thirty seconds. A confirmed sample plays on for up to six seconds more until it holds twelve frames, inside its own deadline; fewer than six frames give no fingerprint, and a page that could not read its frames says so. Two sequences are compared as acoustic fingerprints are, at every alignment within eight frames, by the mean share of differing bits over the frames that overlap, at least six; at or under 0.2 they are the same film, with the identity method `frame_fingerprint` and the verdicts in the film's words. The same film from two hosts differs by a few bits a frame and by at most a frame of alignment, so a logo one host kept and another cut is found within the search. The console bridge hands the fingerprint to Atlas with the length the browser read, as it does for recordings; the library compares it with every film sampled before, joins and keeps apart by it, revisits earlier verdicts, and the movie cards show the fingerprint and its verdict beside the sample.

**The sandbox render.** Pictures and documents reached identified on the ladder, by a probe that decoded a header or parsed a PDF, and never rendered, which only playback samples gave. The same throwaway Chromium and the same local harness that sample a movie now render a picture into an image element, or frame a PDF, through Shield, with every request intercepted and only the harness page and its one file allowed (`internal/scout/render.go`). The harness takes a kind: the page, the policy (`img-src` for a picture, `frame-src` for a document, and no policy on the framed PDF itself, which the browser's own viewer draws) and the bytes the relay admits (a sniffed raster image, never an SVG, or a PDF) follow it, and the playback kind is unchanged. A picture is rendered when the element decoded it and its pixels could be drawn onto the hash grid; the drawn pixels are hashed as the probe hashed the file, so the render also says whether what the sandbox saw is what the probe read. A document is rendered when the frame loaded a PDF document and a screenshot of the sandbox shows a drawn page, measured as the share of pixels more than 24 grey levels off the dominant colour, at least two percent; a frame that loaded without proof of drawing is unconfirmed, not rendered, and a file the relay refused is not rendered and says why. Up to two files a search are rendered, from the thumbnail for pictures (an original may be far larger than the harness relays) and the PDF for documents, two at a time inside the search's budget, in twelve seconds each at most. The console bridge records a render as evidence, rendered when drawn and reachable when the file answered; the merged list raises a drawn file to rendered; the picture and document cards say what the sandbox drew, and the coverage counts the renders attempted and done. Two things the live runs taught: Chromium draws a framed PDF in its own viewer frame, a child target the sample's isolation rule would pause and close, so a document render leaves that one frame, and only that one, running and lets it load its resources from its own origin; and a document search spends its whole common window probing six PDFs at both ends with the OCR text beside each, so the console gives document searches ninety seconds where every other kind has forty-four (the server sets no write timeout), and the harness serving a document lives thirty-two seconds rather than fourteen, since a megabyte of a PDF and its tail take ten of them from the Archive on this connection.

**Conversion for the reader.** The reader framed only PDFs; an EPUB, a MOBI, a DjVu scan or an office file was named as not rendered and sent to the protected browser. `internal/convert` finds the converters a host has, Calibre's `ebook-convert` for e-books, DjVuLibre's `ddjvu` for DjVu and LibreOffice's `soffice` for office files (a path in `ITEMBA_EBOOK_CONVERT`, `ITEMBA_DDJVU` or `ITEMBA_SOFFICE` wins, then the PATH, then the usual Windows install directories), runs one in a scratch directory with a timeout and a pared-down environment, with Calibre's configuration and LibreOffice's profile kept in the scratch so neither touches the host's, checks that what came out is a PDF, and keeps it in a cache bounded at half a gigabyte, oldest out first, keyed by the input's digest, so a file is converted once. Nothing fetches in that package and nothing installs anything: a host without a converter says so. The console's `GET /api/convert?url=<file>` fetches the file through Shield within 48 MiB, converts it and serves the PDF as `application/pdf` with ranges, which is what the reader checks before framing; with `info=1` it converts and answers JSON saying how it went, by which tool and in how long, or why not. The reader asks that first for a convertible format, shows the conversion in its toolbar and frames the converted PDF, or says why this host cannot and offers the protected browser. The sandbox render of a document takes those formats too: the file is fetched and converted on this host and the harness serves the converted PDF in place of a relayed source, so an EPUB can reach rendered on the ladder. The route and the render are tested with a fake tool that writes a fixed PDF; the real converters are host programs the suite never needs. The Archive resolver now names an item's EPUB, MOBI or DjVu as its file when the item has no PDF and no text, so such an item reaches the probe (which reports the format as unsupported), the reader and, on a host with a converter, the sandbox.

Verified on 20 October 2026 on the 8081 console, in two states of the host, which has no converter installed (Windows and the Debian WSL2 host were both checked; nothing was installed by this work). Without a converter, the route answered an Archive EPUB with `supported: false` and the sentence that names the three tools and says none is installed, the plain request answered 503, a `.txt` address 400, and the reader, given the EPUB by its address box, showed that sentence with the protected-browser button in its place. With a stand-in tool named in `ITEMBA_EBOOK_CONVERT`, a small program that takes Calibre's arguments and writes a one-page PDF naming itself a stand-in, the route fetched the 2 MB EPUB from the Archive through Shield, ran the stand-in in a scratch directory, kept the 805-byte PDF in the cache, served it as `application/pdf` with ranges (206 for the reader's first-byte probe, 200 for the whole), answered the second request from the cache, and the reader framed the converted PDF with "Converted from EPUB by ebook-convert earlier" in its toolbar, the cache having kept the PDF from the route's first run. That proves the plumbing; a real conversion waits for a converter on the host, which is the next step and the user's call.
